Scholars called the Hittites fiction — a Bronze Age kingdom the Bible placed alongside Egypt as a military power, with no ruins to confirm it.

In 1906, a German archaeologist named Hugo Winckler began digging near a village in central Turkey called Bogazköy. Within weeks, his team had pulled 10,000 cuneiform tablets from the rubble of a palace.

But this was not a minor tribe. The tablets described an empire that had controlled modern Turkey and Syria for five centuries, fielded 3,500 chariots at the Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BCE, and signed a peace agreement with Ramesses II — one of the oldest surviving written treaties in human history.

The Hittites had been Egypt's actual military equal. An entire civilization, erased from memory for 3,000 years.

Hattusa, their capital, still stands 150 kilometers east of Ankara — stone walls, empty city.
